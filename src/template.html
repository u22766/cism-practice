Lessons

The CISM Mindset

Read this first

CISM does not test whether you can do security work. It tests whether, given a scenario, you make the decision an information security manager would make. Questions are deliberately written so two answers look technically defensible — and the credited one is always the governance-minded, business-aligned choice.

The five questions to ask on every scenario

  1. What is the business objective? Revenue, uptime, compliance, trust — security exists to protect these.
  2. Who owns the decision? Board, senior management, asset owner, steering committee. Rarely you personally.
  3. What is the first management step? The default sequence is assess → communicate → prioritize → document → act.
  4. What is the cost–benefit logic? Control cost versus expected loss and business impact.
  5. What process is supposed to be followed? Policy, IR plan, BIA, change control, contract, SLA.
PrincipleIf your chosen answer skips steps 1–4 and jumps straight to implementing the technical thing, it is usually wrong.

Reading the answer options

  • Options containing align with business objectives, risk assessment first, business case, senior management approval, or escalate to the risk owner are frequently correct.
  • Options where you act unilaterally, fix something immediately without assessment, or make a decision that belongs to the business are frequently wrong — even when they are technically sound.
  • Watch the qualifier: FIRST, BEST, MOST, PRIMARY. Several options may be things you would eventually do; only one is the credited first or best.

The one deliberate exception

When the scenario states that damage is actively spreading right now — ransomware encrypting files, a worm propagating — containment comes before analysis. Stop the bleeding, then investigate. The exam rewards judgment, not a memorized rule, so read whether the threat is active or historical.

Traps for CISSP holders The fix-it reflex (remediate before the owner decides) · assuming you are the decision-maker (the asset owner accepts risk, not security) · deploying controls without a business case · answering in ISC² vocabulary instead of ISACA's · treating governance questions as technology questions.

Information Security Governance

Domain 1 · 17%

Governance is about direction, authority, and accountability — never about controls. If a Domain 1 question tempts you with a technical answer, it is almost certainly wrong.

Governance vs. management

Governance directs, monitors, and evaluates: it sets risk appetite, approves strategy, and checks that outcomes match intent. Management plans, builds, and runs: it executes the program within that direction. Keep the layers straight — many questions test exactly this boundary.

Strategy

  • The primary input to the security strategy is the organization's business objectives and strategy. Threats, benchmarks, and technology inform execution, not direction.
  • Strategy is reviewed periodically and on significant change — a merger, a new market, a new regulation each trigger realignment.
  • In multiple jurisdictions, legal and regulatory requirements are a dominant strategy driver: they are non-negotiable boundaries.

Structures and accountability

  • The board and senior management are ultimately accountable for information security and approve risk appetite. Accountability cannot be delegated to the CISO.
  • A steering committee aligns security with business needs and prioritizes across stakeholders.
  • A security charter formally establishes the program's mandate, authority, and scope with senior endorsement.
  • The CISO reporting outside IT preserves independence from delivery pressure; internal audit stays independent of security to avoid self-review.
  • The best evidence of senior commitment is resources and active participation — not signatures on documents.

The document hierarchy

PolicyHigh-level statement of management intent. Mandatory.
StandardMandatory, measurable specifics that support policy (e.g., 14-character minimum passwords).
ProcedureStep-by-step instructions for performing a task.
GuidelineAdvisory good practice. Not mandatory.

Policy exceptions are risk decisions: assessed, approved at the right level, time-bound, documented, and reviewed at expiry. Expired-but-active exceptions are a governance failure.

Reporting upward

Boards want trends in risk posture relative to appetite, tied to business objectives — not vulnerability counts or tool inventories. A balanced scorecard links security measures to strategy across multiple perspectives.

The governance hierarchy

Direction flows top-down; feedback flows bottom-up. Strategy is formulated in the top five layers and implemented in the bottom three. The exam tests that each layer derives from the one above — policy from strategy, never from tools or existing procedures.

1 · Vision → 2 · MissionTop-level management. Why the organization exists and what it aims to become.
3 · Objectives → 4 · Strategy → 5 · PolicyMiddle management. Objectives set targets; strategy is the route; policy states management's intent for how security will support it.
6 · Program → 7 · Budgets → 8 · ProceduresLower-level management. Implementation: the program executes strategy, budgets fund it, procedures tell people exactly what to do.
Why "policy FIRST"Governance implementation requires approved policy before awareness, response teams, or architecture — those all exist to fulfill policy.

The six governance outcomes

Know these as a set: strategic alignment, risk management, value delivery (investment optimized to support objectives), resource management, performance measurement, and assurance process integration (audit, compliance, ERM, and security converging to close gaps and remove duplication). Questions describe a situation and ask which outcome it demonstrates.

Measures, structures, and decisions

  • KGI vs. KPI: a key goal indicator shows whether a strategic goal was achieved (outcome); a key performance indicator shows how well the process is performing toward it (tactical). KRI warns that risk is rising.
  • Centralized security management gives consistency and stronger policy adherence at lower administrative cost; decentralized gives closer alignment with business-unit needs and faster local response. Trade-off questions pick one attribute.
  • Strategy constraints to recognize: legal, physical, ethical, cultural, cost, and personnel (resistance to change).
  • Decision pitfalls named in the manual: overconfidence, optimism, anchoring, status quo bias, mental accounting, false consensus — and, since 2024, automation bias toward AI outputs. AI-generated analysis is an input the manager validates, not a decision the manager delegates.
  • Ethics now includes AI: governance of AI means defined principles, oversight, transparency, and a human who stays accountable for AI-driven decisions.
Framework currencyThe current manual references COBIT 2019 and ISO/IEC 27001:2022, and replaced the Business Model for Information Security (BMIS) with the Digital Trust Ecosystem Framework (DTEF). Older guides still cite COBIT 5, ISO 27001:2013, and BMIS — the concepts carry over, the names do not.
TrapWhen a governance question offers "deploy technology," "hire staff," or "run a test" as the FIRST step of anything strategic — the credited answer is establishing alignment, structure, or accountability instead.

Information Security Risk Management

Domain 2 · 20%

Domain 2 rewards two things: precise vocabulary and knowing who decides what. Security identifies, analyzes, and recommends. The business decides.

Vocabulary precision

ThreatAn actor or event that could cause harm.
VulnerabilityA weakness that a threat can exploit (e.g., a missing patch).
RiskThe potential for loss when threat, vulnerability, and impact combine.
Inherent riskRisk level before any controls are applied.
Residual riskRisk remaining after controls. Compared against appetite.
Risk appetiteStrategic: how much risk the organization is willing to take in pursuit of objectives. Approved by the board.
Risk toleranceTactical: the acceptable variation around appetite for a specific objective or metric.
KRIKey risk indicator — a forward-looking early warning that exposure is rising.

The process

  1. Identify risks; record them in the risk register with owners, treatments, and status.
  2. Analyze: qualitative (ratings — fast, works without hard data) or quantitative (financial — supports cost-benefit; ALE = SLE × ARO).
  3. Treat: mitigate, transfer, avoid, or accept. Treatment continues until residual risk is within appetite.
  4. Monitor: KRIs with thresholds between assessments; reassess periodically and on significant change.

Ownership — the exam's favorite theme

  • The risk owner (business/asset owner) accepts residual risk and decides treatment. Never security, never the CISO.
  • The control owner keeps a specific control operating effectively day to day.
  • When a business unit leaves a risk beyond appetite untreated, the manager escalates with a business impact analysis — not force, not silent acceptance.
  • Accepted risk is documented with rationale, approving authority, and a review date.

Judgment rules

  • Cost-benefit: a control should not cost more than the exposure it addresses. If it does, find an alternative or recommend acceptance.
  • Assess before acting: shadow IT, BYOD, new regulations, new technology — the first step is always a risk or gap assessment, then a decision with the business.
  • Insurance transfers money, not accountability: reputational damage and ultimate responsibility stay with the organization.
  • Third parties are assessed before the contract is signed, while leverage still exists.
  • Aggregate several small risks on the same critical asset — together they may exceed acceptable levels.

Threat intelligence and threat modeling (added 2024)

  • Threat intelligence is a risk-management input for the manager: it keeps likelihood estimates and prioritization grounded in the real, current threat landscape. Automated blocking in the SOC is a by-product, not the purpose.
  • Threat modeling systematically identifies threats and attack paths against a system — done early in design so controls are built in rather than bolted on.
  • Threat categories to recognize: natural, unintentional, intentional physical, intentional non-physical (fraud, espionage, malware, social engineering, denial of service).

Emerging threats (added 2024)

  • Shadow IT: assess exposure first; the durable fix is understanding the unmet need and offering sanctioned alternatives with discovery and policy — blocking alone drives it underground.
  • AI in use: acceptable-use rules tied to data classification (what may be entered), plus human review of outputs.
  • AI-enabled attacks: deepfake voice and hyper-targeted phishing defeat email filters; the effective control is procedural — out-of-band verification for sensitive requests — backed by awareness.

More risk vocabulary the exam expects

Aggregate riskMany individually minor exposures on one asset combining into a significant one.
Cascading riskOne failure triggering a chain reaction through dependencies.
Semi-quantitative analysisNumeric scales applied to qualitative ratings — ranking without full financial data.
Total cost of ownershipFull life-cycle cost of a control (operation, maintenance, retirement), not purchase price, in every cost-benefit comparison.
Treatment termsCurrent ISACA wording: mitigate, accept, avoid, transfer/share. Older guides say terminate, transfer, mitigate, tolerate — same four ideas.
Residual-risk acceptance depends onRegulation, policy, asset sensitivity and criticality, acceptable impact, assessment uncertainty, and cost-effectiveness of further treatment.
  • Identification methods: team brainstorming builds commitment; structured techniques (flowcharting, system analysis) suit defined processes; scenario and what-if analysis suits strategic, not-yet-defined risks.
  • Impact is the bottom line of risk; it is often easier to reduce potential impact than to reduce the vulnerability itself.
  • Privacy regulation is primarily about identifiable personal data. Outsourced security services must never become a single point of failure — keep oversight, contingency, and an exit path. Key SLA clauses: right to audit, right to review processes, defined SOPs, right to assess vendor skills, advance notice of staffing changes.
Trap"Implement the control immediately" is the practitioner answer. The manager's sequence is assess → inform the owner → let the owner decide — even when the risk is high.

Information Security Program

Domain 3 · 33% — the largest

Domain 3 makes program management itself the subject: turning strategy into a running machine of controls, people, metrics, and money. It is the biggest domain and, for most CISSP holders, the least familiar territory.

The foundation chain

Everything builds in order: asset inventory (you cannot protect what you do not know exists) → classification (protection proportionate to value and sensitivity) → ownership (accountability for access and classification decisions) → standards and baselines (consistent secure state, drift detection) → controls → metrics. Questions about "what comes first" almost always walk this chain — DLP starts with classification, not tooling.

Controls and money

  • Controls are selected because they address an identified risk cost-effectively — not because the technology is new or the vendor is famous.
  • Control effectiveness is established by testing against the objective, never by the control merely existing.
  • Funding is won with a business case tying spend to risk reduction and business value.
  • With constrained resources, prioritize by risk reduction and business value; cover gaps with automation and managed services. Accountability for outsourced functions is always retained.

Metrics

  • Define what decisions the metrics must support before choosing any metric or dashboard.
  • KPI = how well the program performs (percentage patched within SLA). KRI = early warning that risk is growing (overdue critical vulnerabilities climbing).
  • Outcomes beat outputs: 100% training completion with rising user-caused incidents means the training failed, not the users.

People controls

  • Least privilege limits the impact of compromise; periodic access recertification by owners cures privilege creep; segregation of duties prevents one person committing and concealing fraud.
  • Termination = timely revocation of all access. Screening depth matches role sensitivity.
  • Awareness is role-based (developers get secure coding); phishing simulations measure and improve, never punish; effectiveness is measured by behavior change.
  • Insider-threat work always involves HR and legal.

Third parties and the SDLC

  • Security requirements go into the RFP and contract from the start — with a breach-notification clause and right to audit. SOC 2 reports give independent assurance but never replace your own risk assessment.
  • In SaaS, the customer always retains data, access, and configuration responsibility. Watch for fourth parties — your vendors' vendors.
  • Security enters the SDLC at requirements; in DevOps it is automated into the pipeline with early design involvement, not bolted on as a manual gate.

Controls, precisely

  • Technology is not a control — technology implements controls. A purchased firewall satisfies nothing until it is configured, operated, and monitored to enforce the requirement, and its effectiveness verified.
  • Control categories: preventive, detective, corrective (restore after the fact — e.g., backup restoration), deterrent (discourage), and compensating (alternative means to the same objective when the primary control cannot be applied — documented as an exception).
  • Control technology categories: native (built into business systems, run by IT), supplemental (add-ons such as network intrusion detection), management support (SIEM, compliance scanners, event analysis — often run by security itself).
Expect some technical itemsCISM does ask a minority of control-selection questions with technical answers — IDS on a screened subnet, packet filtering against denial of service, patch management for OS weaknesses, role-based access to prevent internal modification, automatic expiry for contractor accounts, tuning as the key IDS consideration. The manager still reasons from risk, but the credited option is the specific technical control. A countermeasure is a targeted control for a specific threat; a general control (such as enterprise MFA) is broader and costlier — the qualifier tells you which is wanted.

Running the program

  • Program objectives: execute the strategy cost-effectively, maximize support of business functions, minimize business disruption.
  • Program components: operational (ongoing SOPs and administration), management (policy reviews, standards development, oversight — less frequent, senior-owned), administrative (finance, HR), educational (awareness, training).
  • A sound business case is adaptable, consistent, business-oriented, comprehensive, understandable, measurable, transparent, and accountable. Features without quantified benefits cannot be compared or evaluated.
  • Budgets include staff time, contractors, equipment, space, testing, documentation, ongoing maintenance, and contingency — maintenance is the element most often underestimated.
  • The three perennial challenges: management support, funding, staffing. A program that is highly technical and tactically driven with few documented management processes is showing weak management components.
  • Roles and responsibilities matrix makes accountability explicit and removes gaps and overlaps. Records retention is driven by legal and regulatory requirements first.
  • Architecture is an articulation of policy — business-focused, layered from contextual to physical, broader than technology (SABSA, COBIT, ISO/IEC 27001 support it). Classification works when it has few, clearly defined levels with explicit handling rules assigned by owners.
  • AI-enabled supply chain (added 2024): when a provider introduces AI into processing your data, extend the third-party risk assessment to data handling, model transparency, and accountability, and update the contract.
Trap"Deploy the tool" is never the first step of any program initiative. Classification before DLP, requirements before procurement, objectives before metrics, business case before budget.

Incident Management

Domain 4 · 30%

CISM tests incident management as a management function: readiness, coordination, communication, and learning. The technical response happens inside a structure the manager built beforehand.

Readiness — built before anything burns

  • The IR plan's most important content: roles, responsibilities, escalation paths, and communication procedures.
  • Classification criteria and escalation thresholds are predefined, based on business impact — severity is never invented mid-crisis.
  • The team is cross-functional: legal, HR, communications, and business representatives alongside security. Playbooks speed common scenarios; a forensics retainer buys instant specialist capacity.
  • Plans are proven by testing: tabletops rehearse decisions cheaply; full interruption tests are realistic but risky and need senior approval. Backups are validated only by test restorations.

BIA, RTO, RPO

BIAIdentifies critical processes and sets their recovery priorities and objectives. The foundation of continuity planning.
RTOTarget time to restore a process after disruption. Drives site strategy: hot site for hours, cold site for days.
RPOMaximum tolerable data loss. Drives backup/replication frequency: a 1-hour RPO means at least hourly backups.
Recovery orderDetermined by BIA criticality — most critical first, never easiest first.

The response flow

  1. Detect and triage: validate and prioritize so resources go where impact is.
  2. Assess and classify per the plan — the default FIRST step for a detected incident.
  3. Contain, weighing the business cost of containment against the risk of spread. When damage is actively spreading, containment jumps to the front.
  4. Eradicate and verify the root cause is gone — recovering first reinfects clean systems.
  5. Recover in BIA order; preserve evidence before restoring anything that may matter legally.
  6. Post-incident review: blameless, all stakeholders, and — most important — corrective actions assigned, tracked, and folded back into the plan.

Communication and decisions

  • Regulatory notification: engage legal counsel first to determine obligations and timelines.
  • Public disclosure and ransom payment are senior management decisions with legal advice — never the response team's call.
  • External statements come from a designated spokesperson per the communication plan; internal comms are need-to-know.
  • Insider incidents: coordinate with HR and legal before acting against anyone.
  • Chain of custody whenever evidence may reach legal proceedings; contemporaneous documentation throughout.
  • Invoke BCP at predefined thresholds; crisis management sits above incident management for organization-threatening events.

Structure of incident management

  • Incident handling = detection and reporting → triage → analysis → response. Response is the last stage; it plans and executes mitigation and recovery.
  • Triage on two levels: tactical (predefined criteria) and strategic (business impact).
  • Teams: the incident management team (IMT) is led by the security manager, who is also the interface to the security steering group; the incident response team (IRT) is led by an incident response manager. Models: centralized, distributed, coordinating, outsourced — matched to the organization's structure.
  • Goals: detect quickly, diagnose accurately, manage properly, contain and minimize damage, restore services, determine root cause, prevent recurrence, document and report.

Recovery terms beyond RTO and RPO

SDO — service delivery objectiveMinimum service level that must be restored after an event to meet business requirements.
AIW / MTO — acceptable interruption window, maximum tolerable outageThe longest the business can survive without a function. RTO must be no longer than the AIW.
Recovery sitesHot, warm, cold, mobile, duplicate, mirrored (real-time replication for near-zero RTO/RPO). Choose by RTO/RPO/SDO, proximity, shared hazards, cost.
Reciprocal agreementsCheap but fragile: capacity and compatibility are not guaranteed when needed, and hard to enforce.

Facts the exam tests directly

  • Recovery teams: emergency action (deals with the emergency), damage assessment (assesses extent of damage), emergency management (coordinates and makes key decisions), security (monitors and resolves security issues), relocation (moves operations to the alternate site).
  • Confirm before you contain. A reported breach is validated first; notification, containment, and law-enforcement contact all follow confirmation.
  • Root compromise → rebuild from original media. You cannot find every change an attacker with superuser access made.
  • Forensic image = bit-by-bit copy. File copies miss slack space and deleted data.
  • RPO can make RTO unattainable — a stricter data-loss target lengthens data restoration time. The SDO helps define the RTO, not the reverse; the RTO precedes the MTO.
  • Most common DR plan failure: stale operational information — hence regular review of plan content, not only exercises.
  • Build vs. rent a hot site: decided on cost and on the site not sharing the primary's environmental risk.

Plans, tests, and readiness details

  • Test progression: tabletop walk-through → tabletop with mock scenario → infrastructure and communications → critical applications → end-user involvement → full restoration with unfamiliar personnel → surprise tests. Assurance and cost rise together. An independent observer gives objective evaluation; test at least annually and track every recommendation to closure.
  • Declaration criteria predefined so managers can invoke recovery without debate. Call tree updated on every personnel change and reviewed at least quarterly — vendors, recovery sites, insurers, regulators, law enforcement.
  • Administrators' first response: confirm and scope the incident, take no action that could alter or contaminate evidence.
  • Insurance: business interruption (lost income), equipment and facilities, media reconstruction, extra expense, errors and omissions, fidelity.
  • Common plan failures: lack of management buy-in, mismatch to organizational structure, team turnover, no communication process, an overly complex plan.
TrapThe eradicate-first reflex. Default: follow the plan, assess, classify. Exception: active, spreading damage → contain now. The exam tests whether you can tell which scenario you are in.

Exam Technique & Logistics

Before test day

The format

  • 150 questions, 4 hours — 96 seconds per question at even pace (this app times you at exactly that).
  • Scaled score 200–800, 450 to pass. Not a raw percentage; questions are weighted by difficulty.
  • Some items are unscored pilot questions and you cannot tell which — treat every question as real.
  • Passing the exam is not certification: you also need 5 years of security management experience across at least 3 of the 4 domains (waivers exist), plus the application. You may sit the exam first.

A method for every question

  1. Find the qualifier — FIRST, BEST, MOST, PRIMARY, GREATEST. It changes the correct answer.
  2. Identify the role and the moment: you are the security manager; is this before assessment, before a decision, mid-incident, or after resolution?
  3. Eliminate two — most questions have two clearly weaker options.
  4. Between the final two, choose the more managerial, business-aligned option: the one that assesses, aligns, escalates to the right owner, or follows the defined process.
  5. Name why the loser loses before committing. If you cannot, flag it and move on.

Pacing

  • 90–96 seconds per question. If you are stuck at two minutes, flag and move — momentum matters more than any single item.
  • Budget a final pass for flagged questions. First instincts are right more often than panicked revisions; change an answer only when you can articulate the reason.

Anatomy of an ISACA item

Real items are short. The stem is usually one sentence carrying one capitalized qualifier — MOST, BEST, FIRST, PRIMARY, GREATEST. The four options are brief, grammatically parallel, and similar in length; all four describe legitimate activities, so none can be eliminated as absurd. When a stem ends with a colon, the options complete the sentence in lowercase and end with a period. The task is never "which is right" but "which is best given the qualifier" — and the credited answer is the management answer, not the technical one. This bank follows those conventions exactly, and every question rationalizes all four options, the way the official ISACA question database explains its answers.

Answer-selection heuristics

  • Prefer the most comprehensive answer. "Meet business objectives" beats "meet legal obligations" — the broader answer contains the narrower one. ISACA plants "juicy" narrow distractors deliberately.
  • Trigger words that often mark the credited answer: integrated, holistic, continuous, aligned, business objectives, risk-based, measure effectiveness.
  • Action verbs matter. "Implement and measure effectiveness" outranks "monitor," "establish," or "implement" alone — effectiveness is the outcome the exam rewards.
  • Absolutes are suspect. Options with "always," "never," "all," or "eliminate" are usually wrong.
  • If any part of an option is wrong, the whole option is wrong. Eliminate it.
  • Two opposite options: one of them is often correct. Two near-identical options: usually neither is.
  • It is almost a reading-comprehension exam. Stems are short; the whole question can turn on one word. Read for the qualifier and the trigger word before reading the options.
  • Experience is a liability when it disagrees with ISACA. The credited answer is the ISACA management answer. Notice when your practitioner instinct is pulling you toward the technically satisfying option and let the management answer win.
Beware outdated materialsMany third-party study guides still in circulation were written to the pre-2022 outline, where the domains were weighted roughly 24% governance, 33% risk, 25% program, 18% incident. The current exam is 17 / 20 / 33 / 30. If you allocate study time by the old numbers you will over-study risk and badly under-study incident management, which nearly doubled. Use the old guides for concepts; use the current weights for time. Also: brain-dump sites promising "actual exam questions" carry disputed answers and their use breaches ISACA's candidate agreement — practice questions should train judgment, not memorize items.

Where the shortcuts fail

"Pick the management answer" gets you most of the way. But the exam regularly credits a specific, mechanism-level answer over the generic one. Each of these will feel wrong the first time:

  • What makes role assignments effective? Existing proficiencies — not senior management support.
  • PRIMARY reason to address security at project start? It may affect feasibility — not "it costs more later."
  • BEST metric for awareness training? The number of reported incidents — a rise means people are paying attention.
  • Classification is based on consequences of compromise — not on risk.
  • Compliance with many regulations? Involve the impacted departments and treat regulation as another risk — not "rely on counsel."
  • Most important quality of an incident handler? Coping with stress — not following procedures, which may not exist for a novel incident.
  • A successful DR test is one where critical business processes are duplicated — not one that meets the RTO.
  • BEST process for assessing existing risk level? A security review — not a vulnerability assessment.
  • When risk changes, standards and procedures change; policies should not need to.
  • Gaining management commitment? Show how security supports desired outcomes — not losses and fear.
  • Highest priority in any response? Life safety, before continuity, recovery, or messaging.
  • The manual contains severity criteria — stable things; not phone lists or backup tables that change weekly.
The patternISACA credits the answer that names the actual mechanism, and it punishes the answer that is merely the safest-sounding. Read for what the qualifier is really asking, then pick the option that most precisely causes that result.

Readiness signal

Aim for ~70%+ overall and in every domain on full timed mocks before test day. A weak domain bar on your results screen tells you exactly where to spend the next session — read that domain's lesson, then run its drill.

The one-line summaryOn every question ask: what would a security manager accountable to the business do first — not what would I, the engineer, do?

What ISACA Changed in 2024

Current manual & question pool

In late 2024 ISACA revised the 16th-edition Review Manual and reworked the official question database: every existing item was reviewed and reclassified, and 91 new items were added on emerging technology, bringing the pool to 1,138. The domain weights did not change — but the content you can be asked about did. This is the checklist of what was added, so none of it is a surprise on exam day.

Domain 1 — Governance

  • Ethics and AI: governance of AI-driven decisions — principles, oversight, transparency, human accountability.
  • AI and decision-making: added to the list of decision pitfalls and biases; treat AI output as input to be validated.
  • Digital Trust Ecosystem Framework (DTEF) replaced the Business Model for Information Security (BMIS).
  • ISO/IEC 27000 series updated to ISO/IEC 27001:2022.

Domain 2 — Risk Management

  • New sections on threat intelligence and threat modeling.
  • Emerging threats now cover shadow IT and AI and network security — both AI as an attack tool and AI-based defenses evaluated like any control: by business case and tested effectiveness.

Domain 3 — Security Program

  • A worked information classification example (few levels, clear handling rules, owner-assigned).
  • Outsourcing challenges now include AI-enabled supply chain management — extend vendor risk assessment and contracts to cover AI use.

Domain 4 — Incident Management

  • No new topic sections — but the refreshed question pool includes emerging-technology scenarios (AI, blockchain) framed through incident readiness and response. Apply the same management logic: predefined criteria, cross-functional roles, business-impact triage.
How to use thisEvery new topic still answers to the same mindset: assess before acting, keep a human accountable, tie controls to classification and risk, and manage third parties through assessment and contract. If an AI or emerging-tech question appears, do not reach for a technical answer — reach for the governance one.
